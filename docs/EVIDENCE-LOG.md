# Evidence log

Every clinical, statistical and design claim this system makes, with the source fetched and
the load-bearing sentence quoted. Checked 4 September 2026. This is the appendix to
`docs/DESIGN-REVIEW.md`; the verdicts and the reasoning are there, the paper trail is here.

**Read this first.** The files in `data/knowledge/` say "READ ONLY. Authored by a human."
They were generated by a language model. So this log divides into two very different halves:

- **The figures that carry a citation.** These were checked against the paper and almost all
  of them hold. Whatever produced these files did the cited part carefully.
- **The figures that carry no citation.** About 140 numbers: 45 temporal profile weights, 10
  of the 12 between-person spreads, 64 answer likelihoods, 9 interruption costs, 6
  persistence thresholds and 4 extreme heart rate thresholds. There is nothing to check them
  against, and they were not, as the files claim, a person's judgement.

**How to read the status column**

| | |
|---|---|
| **VERIFIED** | I fetched the source and the quoted sentence says what the repository says it says |
| **MISATTRIBUTED** | the number is real and the paper named is not where it comes from |
| **SCOPE** | the quote is accurate and the source's population or setting is narrower than the use |
| **NOT FOUND** | I could not find a source saying this |
| **NO EVIDENCE EXISTS** | the repository says no published figure exists, and I agree after searching |

## Detection and baseline

### Alavi et al. 2022, *Nature Medicine* 28:175-184 (NightSignal)
<https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-021-01593-2> ·
<https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8240687/>

Supports: `DETECT_YELLOW_BPM = 3.0`, `DETECT_RED_BPM = 4.0`,
`BASELINE_MIN_NIGHTS_PERSONAL = 7`, the personal-median baseline, the "80 percent" figure,
and the decision to keep the forecast out of the belief model.

**VERIFIED.** Quoted from the paper:

> "A_i < M_i + 3: For night i, the average RHR overnight is less than three bpm above the
> baseline"
>
> "A_i >= M_i + 4: For night i, the average RHR overnight is greater than or equal to four
> bpm above the baseline."
>
> "For each individual we use the streaming median of average overnight RHR as the
> baseline, and raise real-time daily alerts as deviations from baseline as defined by the
> alerting state machine."
>
> "The threshold of 3 was chosen since for all participants, the median of fluctuation of
> medians of average RHR overnight over three months was only three bpm."
>
> "for most subjects (over 80%), median of average RHR overnight is a stable and reliable
> baseline, since only after seven nights, it hits a baseline close to the baseline over
> three months."
>
> "The NightSignal method has the highest sensitivity (78%) compared to CuSum (54%) and
> RHRAD (44%), but produces the same rate of false (non-COVID-19) signals."

Also verified against the authors' own implementation at
<https://github.com/StanfordBioinformatics/wearable-infection/blob/main/nightsignal.py>,
which contains `yellow_threshold = 3` and `red_threshold = 4`, filters to hours 00 to 06,
requires `rec_st == "0"` (zero steps in that minute), takes the **mean** of the night, and
uses an **expanding** streaming median including tonight.

**SCOPE, and it matters.** Four of the five choices that define the algorithm differ in
this build: the window, the step filter, mean versus median, and the baseline window. The
red-alert rule is also looser here. The published 78 percent does not transfer. See
`docs/DESIGN-REVIEW.md` for the measurement that replaces it.

### Quer et al. 2020, *PLOS One* 15(2):e0227709
<https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0227709>

Supports: the anchor sentence in `causes.yaml`, "median weekly RHR fluctuation within a

**VERIFIED.** n = 92,457 wearable users over at least 35 weeks. The study computes, per

### Whitehurst, Naji and Mednick 2018, *Neurobiology of Sleep and Circadian Rhythms* 5:52-57
Supports: excluding naps from the night window.

**VERIFIED** by the repository's own check, which I did not repeat: n = 32,

**SCOPE, and the repository already says so.** The quotation is verbatim. The cohort is
eleven adolescent cross-country skiers and biathletes, aged 16 plus or minus 1, and the
paper reports its variability result as *contrary* to its own hypothesis. The corpus passage
states the cohort.

## The twelve causes

### infection, `effect_mean_bpm = 1.60`
**MISATTRIBUTED citation. VALUE CONFIRMED BY MEASUREMENT.** The most important entry here,
and the one where the first draft of the review was wrong.

**The citation is wrong.** `causes.yaml` cites Mishra et al. 2020, Alavi et al. 2022 and
Radin et al. 2021. `docs/CORPUS-VERIFICATION.md` credits the sentence "average peak
increases relative to baseline of 1.8% for females and 3.4% for males" to Mishra et al.
2020. I fetched that paper (<https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9020268/>) and
searched it. **Those percentages are not in it.** What Mishra does report about magnitude is
a median heart rate increase of 7 bpm during detected abnormal periods.

The percentages come from a paper the repository does not cite:

**Natarajan A, Su H-W, Heneghan C. Occurrence of relative bradycardia and relative
tachycardia in individuals diagnosed with COVID-19. *Frontiers in Physiology*
2022;13:898251.** <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9127385/>

> "elevated during symptom onset, with average peak increases relative to the baseline of
> 1.8% (3.4%) for females (males)"

Fitbit COVID-19 survey, 21 May 2020 to 10 June 2021, 7,200 individuals with COVID-19. The
peak occurs roughly 2 to 2.6 days **after** symptom onset.

**The derivation also mixes cohorts**, multiplying that percentage by 62.75 bpm, which is
the mean of 525 first-year university students from a different study.

**But the value itself is right, and I measured it rather than argued about it.** Using this
system's own `compute_baseline`, on the 30 labelled COVID cases, with matched control
windows so the upward bias of taking a highest night is removed:

| Highest night above the person's own baseline, 7 night window | median |
|---|---|
| Healthy people, random 7 night windows (46 windows) | 5.00 bpm |
| The 7 nights before symptom onset (23 cases) | 6.00 bpm |
| **The infection contribution** | **+1.0 bpm** |

Counting only still minutes, the difference is **+1.5 bpm**. `causes.yaml` says 1.60. The
value stands; the citation and the derivation do not.

**The effect has two regimes and the model has one number.** Comparing 15 night windows, the
illness median is 11.0 bpm against a control median of 7.0, and the day-by-day median curve
peaks at **+6.00 bpm on the day after symptom onset**. So roughly 1 to 1.5 bpm before
symptoms and 4 to 6 bpm at and after onset. The model captures the first and understates the
second by about four times.

**The base rate has no source.** `causes.yaml` uses 0.025, which is about nine days of
infection a year. Adults average two to four colds a year lasting seven to eleven days,
which is nearer seven percent of days
(<https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK532961/>). Either cite a source for 0.025 or raise
it.


### alcohol, `effect_mean_bpm = 2.60`, base rate 0.299
**VERIFIED, with two caveats about use.**

Grosicki et al. 2026, *PLOS Digital Health* 5(3):e0001284.
<https://journals.plos.org/digitalhealth/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pdig.0001284>

> "Following eligibility filtering, the analytic sample included 20,968 participants
> (10,025 female and 10,943 male), contributing 5,109,185 person-days of observation."
>
> "consuming one drink more than personal average, compared with one less, was associated
> with an increase in RHR by 2.8 bpm (99.9% CI: 2.7, 2.9) in females and 2.4 bpm (99.9%
> CI: 2.3, 2.4) in males"
>
> "Increases in alcohol intake were associated with dose-dependent increases in RHR and
> decreases in HRV."

1,529,080 of 5,109,185 person-days involved alcohol, which is 29.9 percent.

**Caveat one:** the quoted contrast spans **two drinks** (one above the personal average
against one below), not one. **Caveat two:** the effect is dose dependent and the model has
a single binary alcohol switch, so the three-level answer space is collected and discarded.

### The blood oxygen thresholds, 94 and 92 percent
**MISATTRIBUTED.** `triage_rules.yaml` says "WHO guidance as reported in the wearable SpO2
literature: above 94 percent can be cared for at home, below 93 percent warrants medical
attention."

I could not find a WHO source with those action thresholds. WHO published a provisional
recommendation in January 2021 to **use** home pulse oximetry in COVID-19 management. The
94 and 92 action thresholds are **NHS England and British Thoracic Society** guidance, as
carried in the BMJ remote-monitoring literature:

> "Consider assessment in person... if oxygen saturation is 94% or below (this aligns with
> new guidance from NHS England and the British Thoracic Society)... consider urgent
> referral for hospital assessment if it is 92% or below."

**SCOPE.** Those thresholds are for people **already known to have COVID-19** being monitored
at home. Applying them to a healthy person's overnight screening changes their meaning
entirely.

### Pulse oximetry and skin pigmentation
**VERIFIED for existence, STRONGER THAN SOURCE for the caution.**

*J Med Internet Res* 2024;26:e62769, "Impact of Skin Pigmentation on Pulse Oximetry Blood
Oxygenation and Wearable Pulse Rate Accuracy: Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis".
<https://www.jmir.org/2024/1/e62769>. 23 pulse oximetry studies, 59,684 participants,
197,353 paired SpO2-SaO2 observations, 15.58 percent with dark skin pigmentation.

The review finds pulse oximeters significantly overestimate SpO2 for both light and dark
pigmentation, and concludes that **this overestimation may not be clinically relevant**. The
repository states the caution more strongly than the source. Erring toward caution is the
right direction, and the documentation should not imply the paper says more than it does.

### Draelos et al. 2025, arXiv:2507.18905
<https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.18905>

## The measurement this review performed

### Data
Mishra et al. 2020, *Nature Biomedical Engineering* 4:1208-1220.

> "The de-identified raw heart rate, steps and sleep data used in this study can be
> downloaded from the study data repository
> (https://storage.googleapis.com/gbsc-gcp-project-ipop_public/COVID-19/COVID-19-Wearables.zip)."

378 MB, 281 files, minute-level heart rate and steps for 120 participants, sleep staging for
32, 8,508 nights with overnight heart rate.

### Labels
The same paper's Supplementary Data 1
(`41551_2020_640_MOESM3_ESM.xlsx` from Springer), Supplementary Table 3, columns
`ParticipantID, Category, Symptom_dates, covid_diagnosis_dates, recovery_dates`, headed
**"Dates have been shifted to obscure PHI"**, the same shift applied to the public files. 47

### Method
`tools/cohort/evaluate.py` imports this system's own `compute_nocturnal_rhr`,
`compute_baseline` and `compute_detection_for_features` and replays them night by night per
participant, so what is measured is what the product does. The published NightSignal
algorithm is implemented separately, transcribed from the authors' own code. A random
detector matched to each participant's own alert-run count provides the chance floor, over
2,000 trials.

Every script named in this section used to live in `scratch/`, which `.gitignore` excludes,
so a reader following these instructions on a clean checkout would have found nothing. They
were promoted into `tools/cohort/` on 5 September 2026 for exactly that reason: a conclusion
that cannot be reproduced from the repository is not evidence. Paths below are updated.

### Results

> **SUPERSEDED 5 September 2026.** The headline this section published, 53.3 percent
> sensitivity at a 3-day window against a 20.2 percent chance floor and one investigation
> every 3.6 weeks, was measured while the detector ran a **looser alert rule than the one it
> was citing**: two raised nights with at least one at +4, where NightSignal requires two
> consecutive nights both at +4. Item 8 of the refinement plan tightened it to the published
> rule.
>
> **The current figures**, on the same cohort and the same code, are in
> `docs/detection-results.json` and `EVALUATION.md`: **46.7 percent at a 3-day window
> against a 16.5 percent rate-matched chance floor**, 66.7 against 34.2 at seven days, and
> one investigation per healthy person every **4.9 weeks**. This system and NightSignal now
> agree case for case; what survives is a longer lead time, 3.0 days against 1.5, and a
> lower alert burden. `docs/DESIGN-VS-NIGHTSIGNAL.md` has the account.
>
> The strictly-before-symptoms figure of 43.3 percent has no current equivalent:
> `tools/cohort/report.py` scores four symmetric windows and does not compute it, so rather
> than restate a number the committed tool cannot reproduce, it is withdrawn.

### Four further measurements on the same data

**The infection effect, spread and shape.** `tools/cohort/infection.py`. Reported in the infection entry above. The control matters:
taking the highest night in a window is biased upward, so illness windows are compared with
matched windows in people with no illness label.

**The step filter.** `tools/cohort/band_width.py`. 101 people, every night scored twice,
once over all minutes and once over minutes with zero steps. Night-to-night wobble: **2.97
bpm both ways**, 15 of 101 improved, median change zero. The step filter does nothing here,
because this system uses the median rather than the mean.

**Absolute against personal thresholds.** `tools/cohort/sweep.py`. Both families swept
over the same 120 people, sensitivity in the seven days before symptoms against alert rate on
76 healthy people. The fixed threshold in beats is equal or better at every matched alert
rate. The current 3.0 and 4.0 are confirmed; the persistence rule is the lever worth moving.

**The Chronos forecast.** `tools/cohort/chronos.py`. A
forecast computed for all 7,295 eligible nights. Separating illness nights from ordinary
nights: **residual AUC 0.629, forecast AUC 0.586**. Across 30 labelled infections, the
forecast caught at most one case the detector missed, and none at tighter settings. The two
correlate at 0.73.

### What this measurement does not show
- One virus. COVID-19 only, in 2020, in a US Fitbit-owning cohort.
- 30 positive cases. The confidence interval on 53.3 percent is wide.
- Symptom onset is self-reported and coarse, to the day.
- The detection window choice dominates the headline number, which is why four are reported.
- The sleep window comparison rests on 21 people, of whom only 3 sleep substantially outside
  a midnight-to-07:00 window, so it cannot test the case the design was built for.
- Nothing here evaluates the belief model, the policy, the agent or the conversation. It
  evaluates the detector only.
